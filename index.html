How long would it take to count every password like yours?

A password is one item picked from a very large, countable set. This page counts that set with permutations, combinations and the pigeonhole principle, then turns the count into entropy, guessing time and collision odds. It all runs inside this tab; nothing you type is sent anywhere.

    How many passwords are possible

    Each position holds one character, and characters may repeat. Choosing R symbols for each of L positions is an ordered arrangement with repetition, so there are RL possibilities.

    Change the rules and the count changes sharply. Forbid repeats and it is a permutation, P(R, L). Ignore order and it becomes a combination, C(R, L), which is smaller by a factor of L!. Only the first row is a real password model, but the others show how much of the strength comes from order and repetition.

    When two inputs land in the same slot

    A hash squeezes unlimited inputs into 2b outputs. By the pigeonhole principle, once there are more inputs than outputs two must share one. The birthday problem says a collision shows up far earlier, around the square root of the slot count.

    How it works, and where it is wrong

    Entropy and keyspace

    With alphabet size R and length L there are N = RL equally likely passwords when characters are chosen uniformly at random. Entropy is H = log2 N = L log2 R bits. Each bit doubles the search.

    Guessing time

    An attacker who enumerates in random order needs (N + 1) / 2 guesses on average and N in the worst case. Dividing by guesses per second gives time. The rates in the attacker list are round numbers for a sense of scale and depend heavily on the hash function and hardware.

    Permutations, combinations and inclusion–exclusion

    Ordered with repeats: RL. Ordered without repeats: R! / (R − L)!. Unordered without repeats: R! / (L! (R − L)!). Unordered with repeats: C(R + L − 1, L). A policy that demands at least one character from each of k classes is counted as the sum over subsets S of the classes of (−1)|S| (R − sizes in S)L. The rearrangements of your own characters are L! divided by the factorial of each repeated character's count. All counts are exact big integers.

    Pigeonhole and birthday bounds

    With n inputs and 2b slots, n > 2b forces a collision. For n much smaller, the chance of at least one collision is about 1 − e−n(n−1) / 2b+1. The number of inputs for probability p is about √(2 · 2b · ln(1 / (1 − p))).

    The pattern-aware estimate

    The uniform figure assumes random characters, which people rarely produce. The as-typed estimate splits the password into the cheapest mix of pieces: common passwords by rank, dictionary words (including simple substitutions such as @ for a), years, repeated characters or blocks, alphabet and number sequences, and keyboard runs. Everything else costs log2 R bits per character. The word list here is a small built-in sample, so real attackers with millions of words do better than this model. Treat it as an illustration of the idea, not a substitute for a tool such as zxcvbn.

    Other limits

    Characters outside printable ASCII are counted as a pool of 100, a rough guess. Passwords reused across sites, leaked in earlier breaches, or guessed from personal details are weaker than any of these figures. Long random passphrases and a password manager beat clever substitutions.

    Keyspace is a teaching tool. Time estimates are order-of-magnitude.
